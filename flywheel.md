# The Flywheel: S&P 500 Corporate Stack

## The thesis in one paragraph

A new business needs roughly sixty categories of products and services to operate: cloud, payroll, insurance, shipping, travel, legal, and the rest. Today that spend scatters wherever procurement lands. The S&P 500 Corporate Stack is a reference architecture that maps those sixty needs onto vendors inside the S&P 500, diversified so no single vendor dominates. When a company adopts the stack, its operating budget becomes diversified revenue flowing to S&P 500 vendors. That revenue supports earnings, earnings support shareholder value, and shareholders include the very vendors being asked to certify, list, and promote the stack. Adoption funds the incentive to adopt.

## The loop, step by step

1. **Adoption.** A new business picks the stack instead of assembling vendors ad hoc. The builder in this repo produces the recommendation in seconds: one primary vendor per capability, maximum vendor spread.
2. **Diversified spend.** The company's operating budget, on the order of $5M to $50M per year depending on size, flows to dozens of S&P 500 vendors instead of concentrating in three or four.
3. **Vendor revenue.** Each participating vendor books real, recurring, high-retention B2B revenue. B2B contracts renew; this is the revenue Wall Street pays multiples for.
4. **Shareholder value.** Revenue becomes earnings, earnings support the index. Every S&P 500 shareholder, including index funds holding the whole market, benefits from the demand the stack creates.
5. **Vendor incentive.** Vendors are asked to actualize the model: a "Stack Certified" badge, a marketplace listing inside the stack directory, and default-vendor status in their strongest categories. The pitch to each vendor is not charity. It is a customer acquisition channel aimed at every new business that adopts the stack, plus the signaling value of being the index's recommended vendor in a category.

## Where the loop is strong

- **For the adopting business:** vendor selection is genuinely expensive and slow. A credible, diversified default stack saves months of procurement. That value is real even if the flywheel never spins.
- **For vendors in categories with thin competition inside the index:** if only two S&P 500 companies sell business payroll software, being the stack's default payroll vendor is a durable acquisition moat.
- **For index shareholders:** the spend being directed already exists. The stack does not ask businesses to spend more; it asks them to spend the same dollars through index vendors. The flywheel redirects existing economic energy rather than conjuring new demand.

## Where the loop is narrative, stated honestly

- **The collective-action problem.** Each vendor would prefer the stack recommend it in every category it touches, alone. A diversified stack asks every vendor to accept shared shelf space. The equilibrium only holds if the certification is valuable enough that vendors prefer a slice of a growing pie to no pie at all. That is plausible, not guaranteed.
- **The cost of constrained choice.** A stack that maximizes index coverage is not the same as a stack that maximizes quality or price in each category. Sometimes the best product in a category is not in the index. Adopters who follow the stack blindly will occasionally buy the second-best tool. The honest pitch is "good enough everywhere, diversified always," not "best in class."
- **Coverage is not 500.** A meaningful share of the index sells nothing a business can buy: pure consumer brands, commodity producers, REITs with no enterprise offering. This repo reports real coverage: 278 mapped, 32 indirect (consumer spend usable as employee perks), 190 unmapped. The flywheel runs on the mapped set. Claims of "all 500" would be theater, and theater kills trust with the exact CFOs the stack needs.
- **Revenue directed is not revenue captured.** The builder estimates spend a company would direct toward index vendors. Vendors still have to win and keep the business. The stack is a front door, not a purchase order.

## What vendors are actually asked to do

1. **Certify.** Confirm the mapped products are current and buyable by businesses; keep a contact for stack adopters.
2. **List.** Appear in the stack directory under their categories with accurate product names and business-tier pricing entry points.
3. **Promote.** Reference the stack in partner and startup programs (cloud credits, startup tiers), which is where new businesses actually choose vendors.

In return they get placement in front of every adopting company and the compounding credibility of being the index's default in their category.

## What this is not

It is not an index fund, not investment advice, and not a claim that buying from index vendors moves stock prices directly. Corporate revenue is one input among many into earnings. The flywheel is a demand-routing mechanism with a shareholder-aligned incentive story, published as open data so anyone can check the mappings.
